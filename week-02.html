Key concepts and requirements classification

Requirement against design, and the four classes. Two distinctions that the exam returns to again and again.

The second half of chapter 2 gives you two distinctions worth being exact about. The first is requirement against design. A requirement is a usable representation of a need; a design is a usable representation of a solution. What separates them is intent — not level of detail, not which phase produced them, not the notation.

The second is the classification schema: business, stakeholder, solution — functional and non-functional — and transition. Getting this right matters because the class determines who owns a requirement, how it is validated, and in the case of transition requirements, when it stops applying.

Be precise about these

TermWhat it means
RequirementA usable representation of a need. It says what is needed, not how it will be met.
DesignA usable representation of a solution. Both are elaborated in parallel throughout, and both are legitimate business analysis outputs.
Business requirementA goal or objective of the enterprise. Reducing processing cost by 20% within two years.
Stakeholder requirementThe need of a particular group. Assessors need to see restriction data while assessing.
Solution requirementWhat the solution must do (functional) or how well it must do it (non-functional). A quality with no measure attached is an aspiration, not a requirement.
Transition requirementNeeded only to move from current to future state. It lapses once the change is complete, which is why they should be closed rather than carried forward for years.

What the exam does with this

Judging requirement against design by how detailed it is. A one-line statement can be a design; a page of prose can be a requirement.

Assuming the distinction maps onto project phases. A mock-up produced during analysis is still a design.

Classifying by who raised it. A goal of the enterprise is a business requirement whoever mentions it.

Leaving transition requirements open after go-live, so the repository stops describing anything true.

Before the session

  • Read the rest of chapter 2, particularly the requirements classification schema.
  • Take ten statements from a real requirements document and classify each. Note the ones you argue with yourself about.
  • Find a statement in your own work that is really a design labelled as a requirement.

Worth discussing

  • Where does your organisation draw the line between requirement and design, and does it hold?
  • What happens to transition requirements in your shop after cutover?
  • Has a vocabulary dispute ever been mistaken for a requirements dispute on your work?
